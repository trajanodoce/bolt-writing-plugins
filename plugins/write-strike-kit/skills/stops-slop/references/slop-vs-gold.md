# Slop vs. gold: what AI does to great copy

Real, attributed copy beside the slop an AI would produce for the same brief. The contrast is the lesson: AI strips the specific, the falsifiable, and the unique, then adds a "landscape." Each slop version is tagged with the tells it contains (named per `banned.md` and `structures.md`), so the examples double as pattern drills.

These are famous pieces quoted for teaching. Attribution is noted; we don't claim them.

## How to use this file

- **Pre-draft:** read one pair in your content type's neighborhood to set the bar before you write.
- **Borderline audits (a 30–35 score):** put your weakest line next to a pair. It usually becomes obvious which side of the table it's on.
- **Teaching:** onboarding a teammate or external creator? This file, then the three-rules test, then the catalog. In that order.

## The classics

### Volkswagen, "Lemon" (DDB / Bill Bernbach, 1960)
**Real:**
> This Volkswagen missed the boat. The chrome strip on the glove compartment is blemished and must be replaced. Chances are you wouldn't have noticed it; Inspector Kurt Kroner did. There are 3,389 men at our Wolfsburg factory with only one job: to inspect Volkswagens at each stage of production. We pluck the lemons; you get the plums.

**Slop:**
> At Volkswagen, our cutting-edge inspection process leverages a rigorous, data-driven approach, empowering our quality professionals to deliver vehicles that exemplify our commitment to precision across the automotive landscape.

**Tells:** Tier 1 vocabulary ×4 (cutting-edge, leverages, empowering, landscape) · copula inflation ("exemplify") · significance inflation ("commitment to precision") · zero names, zero numbers.
**What the slop killed:** the inspector's name, the number (3,389), the parenthetical aside, and the closing line that was the whole ad.

### Rolls-Royce (Ogilvy, 1958)
**Real:**
> At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock.

**Slop:**
> Experience unparalleled automotive excellence, where timeless elegance meets cutting-edge engineering.

**Tells:** Tier 1 (cutting-edge) · Tier 3 pileup (unparalleled, timeless) · vague declarative (excellence asserted, never shown) · imperative "Experience" opener.
**What the slop killed:** the most specific, falsifiable, unforgettable claim in advertising history, traded for an atmospheric noun.

### Apple iPod (2001)
**Real:**
> 1,000 songs in your pocket.

**Slop:**
> A revolutionary portable device that empowers music enthusiasts to leverage an extensive library with substantial onboard storage capacity.

**Tells:** Tier 1 (empowers, leverage) · Tier 3 (revolutionary) · elegant variation dodging the plain words ("substantial onboard storage capacity" for "1,000 songs").
**What the slop killed:** six words that pass all three rules, swapped for "substantial onboard storage capacity."

### The Economist (David Abbott, 1988)
**Real:**
> "I never read The Economist." Management trainee. Aged 42.

**Slop:**
> Stay informed in today's fast-paced world. The Economist is the trusted source for forward-thinking professionals at every stage of their careers.

**Tells:** dead AI language ("in today's fast-paced world") · false breadth ("at every stage of their careers") · Tier 3 (trusted, forward-thinking) · the joke surgically removed.
**What the slop killed:** twelve words that argue by implication, plus the punchline (the age), traded for sixty that argue nothing.

### Avis, "We Try Harder" (DDB, 1962)
**Real:**
> Avis is only No. 2 in rent a cars. So why go with us? We try harder. We can't afford dirty ashtrays. Or half-empty gas tanks. Or worn wipers.

**Slop:**
> Excellence in every journey. At Avis, we are committed to a customer experience that exemplifies our dedication to service.

**Tells:** significance inflation ("committed to," "dedication to") · copula inflation ("exemplifies") · vague declarative (which experience? shown by what?) · the confession deleted.
**What the slop killed:** the bravery. Slop can't say "we're No. 2." Within a year of the campaign, Avis went from losing $3.2 million to a $1.2 million profit, its first in over a decade, and its share climbed from 11% toward the mid-30s over the next four years (Slate).

## The modern wing

### Slack (2014)
**Real:**
> Be less busy.

**Slop:**
> Slack is a comprehensive collaboration platform that empowers teams to seamlessly streamline communication and enhance productivity across the organization.

**Tells:** Tier 1 ×3 (empowers, seamlessly, streamline) · Tier 2 cluster (comprehensive, enhance) · could describe any of forty products (fails the competitor test).
**What the slop killed:** the promise. Three words aimed at the actual pain (busyness), replaced by a feature-shaped sentence aimed at nothing.

### FedEx (Ally & Gargano, 1978)
**Real:**
> When it absolutely, positively has to be there overnight.

**Slop:**
> FedEx delivers reliable, expedited shipping solutions designed to meet the evolving logistics needs of businesses in today's fast-paced marketplace.

**Tells:** dead AI language ("today's fast-paced") · Tier 1 (ever-evolving's cousin "evolving") · "solutions" · zero commitment (nothing falsifiable survives).
**What the slop killed:** the guarantee. The real line stakes the whole brand on one measurable promise; the slop offers "solutions." Note the real line even keeps two adverbs, and earns them: rules serve writing.

### Basecamp homepage (c. 2019) — the structure-level lesson
**Real (the device, condensed):**
> **Before Basecamp:** Projects feel scattered, things slip, and it's hard to see where things stand. **After Basecamp:** Everything's organized in one place, you're on top of things, and a sense of calm sets in.

**Slop:**
> Basecamp offers a comprehensive suite of project management features, including to-do lists, message boards, schedules, and file storage, empowering teams to collaborate more effectively.

**Tells:** feature dump (structure-level slop: the list replaces the argument) · Tier 1 (empowering) · Tier 2 (comprehensive) · no before, no after, no reader.
**What the slop killed:** the reader's own timeline. Before/after IS the argument (it's the JTBD push-pull made visible); a feature list is an inventory. Structure can carry the persuasion or destroy it. This is why blog posts die when they become "Introduction / What is X / Benefits of X / Conclusion."

### Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946) — the prose opening
**Real:**
> Most people who bother with the matter at all would admit that the English language is in a bad way, but it is generally assumed that we cannot by conscious action do anything about it.

**Slop:**
> In today's rapidly evolving communication landscape, language plays a crucial role in how we connect, collaborate, and share ideas. In this essay, we'll explore some of the key challenges facing modern English and unpack actionable strategies for improvement.

**Tells:** dead AI language ("in today's...") · Tier 1 (landscape, unpack, actionable) · Tier 2 (crucial, key) · rule of three ("connect, collaborate, and share") · meta-commentary ("In this essay, we'll explore") · agrees with everyone about everything.
**What the slop killed:** the position. Orwell opens by picking a fight with an assumption; the slop opens by announcing a topic. A blog intro that would survive this table states something a reader could disagree with, in the first sentence.

## Gold that breaks the rules

The catalog catches the model's defaults, not the writer's decisions. Famous lines that our own filter would flag, and why they stay:

- **M&M's (1954): "Melts in your mouth, not in your hands."** A binary contrast, the exact structure `structures.md` bans. It stays because the negation IS the product claim: falsifiable (hold one), specific (your hands), and unique (only the candy with the shell can say it). The banned pattern is a *default reached for drama*; this is a *tested claim that happens to be a contrast*.
- **The Economist's fragments: "Management trainee. Aged 42."** Dramatic fragmentation, banned on sight. It stays because the fragments are the punchline's timing, not manufactured profundity.
- **FedEx's adverbs: "absolutely, positively."** We kill adverbs. These two are the promise's spine, and the rhythm is the guarantee.

The doctrine (mr-gay's law): break any rule before saying something barbarous. If you can defend the line the way you'd defend a claim, with intent and a reason, it stays. If the pattern arrived because it's what the machine reaches for, it dies.

## The repair drill: slop → gold

The examples above run downhill (real → slop). Your job runs uphill. Here's the direction of travel, moves named:

**Slop:**
> In today's fast-paced digital landscape, TaskFlow serves as a comprehensive solution that empowers teams to seamlessly streamline their workflows, enhancing productivity and fostering collaboration across the organization.

**The moves:**
1. **Delete what survives deletion.** "In today's fast-paced digital landscape" modifies nothing. Gone.
2. **Un-inflate the copula.** "Serves as a comprehensive solution" = "is." Now the sentence must say what it IS.
3. **Demand the missing specifics.** Empowers whom? Streamlines what? By how much? The slop has no answer, which is the real finding: this sentence knows nothing. Go get a fact.
4. **Rebuild from the fact.** (Numbers below invented for the drill.)

**Gold:**
> TaskFlow turns your Monday status meeting into a two-minute dashboard check. Beta teams cut weekly planning from three hours to twenty minutes.

A name, a number, a claim you could be wrong about. Same product, opposite sentence.

**Your turn (unrepaired, for practice or teaching):**
> Our platform leverages cutting-edge AI to deliver actionable insights that help stakeholders navigate an increasingly complex business environment, ensuring meaningful outcomes at every stage of the journey.

## What these teach

AI strips the specific (3,389 becomes "dedicated professionals"), the falsifiable (the electric clock becomes "unparalleled"), and the unique ("we're No. 2" becomes "excellence in every journey"). It reaches for "landscape," "journey," "commitment to," and "cutting-edge." When your draft leans on those, you're one sentence from slop. The fix is always the same: a name, a number, or a claim you could be wrong about.
